Scrambled Brains

How accurate must a wiring map be before a brain built from it keeps its behaviour? We run a whole fruit-fly nervous system from its connectome, teach it to ride a bicycle, then corrupt the wiring step by step, from realistic mapping errors to random wiring, and measure where the riding breaks.

  • 166,700 neurons, brain and nerve cord
  • 25.6 M connections, 124 M synapses
  • wiring ladder R0 to R5
  • one task the bike cannot do by itself

The question

Whole-brain emulation assumes that a scanned wiring map plus a neuron model reproduces behaviour. Proofreading the map is projected to dominate the cost of future connectomes, yet nobody has measured how much wiring accuracy a closed-loop behaviour actually needs.

The sharpest objection is the "digital sphinx": Brunton et al. (eLife 2026) trained a decoder that let a 302-neuron worm connectome drive a walking fly body, and argued that a random network would have served as well. If a trained decoder can extract behaviour from scrambled wiring, closed-loop behaviour is not a valid test of an emulation at all.

So we grade the fly's wiring from real to random and score every rung on identical tasks: riding a bicycle below the speed at which it balances itself, the model's published decoder-free predictions, and how many neurons a decoder must read. The result is a fidelity curve: an acceptance test any connectome or emulator can be run against.

The wiring ladder

Every rung keeps the neurons and each connection's sender, so out-degrees and transmitter signs stay where they are. What else survives shrinks rung by rung. Generators and tests: fly-brain, brain/variants.py.

Results so far

Three things so far. The real wiring can balance the bike below its self-stable speed, so the task is not solved by the bicycle. The pre-test shows which rungs still carry a left-right signal, and it puts the break between two rungs that differ only in who a neuron's partners are. And the first rewired brains are now learning to ride.

    2. The transmission pre-test: do scrambled brains still carry a left-right signal?

    Before any rung gets search compute, we hold the bike upright, drive every sense at cruise rate, and add 30 Hz to the left or the right vertical-system (VS) or haltere inputs, in 30 brains per cell. A cell passes when, for both probes, more descending and motor neurons tell left from right (|z| > 3) than in any of 1,000 shuffled groupings, at a network rate within 2× of the real brain's (10,750 to 43,000 spikes/s). The synapse scale multiplies Shiu's weight; 0.47 is the male CNS calibration.

    The real wiring and the three mildest rungs pass at the calibrated scale: transmitter errors (R1), count jitter (R2) and type-preserving rewiring (R3) give 31 to 50 lateralised outputs where shuffled groupings give at most 13. R3 does it at 73% of the real network rate, so wiring specified only down to cell types still carries the bike's left-right signal. One step down, R3.5 gives every neuron the right number of inputs, the right input strength and the right excitatory/inhibitory mix, from the wrong partners, and fails at every scale. Degree-preserving wiring (R4) and random wiring (R5) are silent at the calibrated scale. R4 carries a signal at 1.5 times Shiu's weight but stays just under the rate window; R5 passes at twice Shiu's weight with a weak signal (7 and 8 outputs; the real wiring has 38 and 39). That pass contradicts our draft prediction that random wiring fails everywhere. One rewiring draw per rung. Cells marked 8 are the earlier 8-brain pilot.

    Between the ladder steps: is a failing rung only miscalibrated?

    No, for R3.5. At 0.50 its network rate equals the real brain's and it still carries no left-right signal: one lateralised output, while single brains burst so much that shuffled groupings reach 160. A signal above the shuffles appears only from 0.6, at more than five times the real rate. R4 has the opposite problem: a clear signal at 1.2 and 1.5, then runaway activity at 1.6.

    The pre-test holds the bike upright, and riding drives the senses harder. The real wiring's activity rises five times once the bike moves; R4's rises 13 to 19 times, and above 1.3 it runs away: at 1.5 every R4 brain reached 4 million spikes/s within 0.4 s of setting off, before any bike fell. So a rung's synaptic scale has to be set while riding, not upright. For R4 that scale is 1.2, where its activity matches the real brain's, and that is where its pilot ride runs.

      Three ways to score a rung

      Track A · closed-loop riding

      A decoder learned from blank weights (cross-entropy method, identical seeds for every rung) reads the k descending neurons that the brain's own passenger screen ranks highest, k = 8 and 32, with a random-k control. Reported as the gap to two reference lines: a decoder that bypasses the brain and reads the senses directly (ceiling), and the hands-off bike (floor).

      Track B · decoder-free predictions

      Shiu et al.'s published feeding and grooming predictions and a handful of flybench tasks, rerun on every rung with no trained readout. Adds transmitter errors at Eckstein et al.'s measured confusion rates as a control the field does not yet have.

      Track C · decodability

      How many recorded neurons a decoder needs to reach 90% of the full roll-rate R² (N90), at a brain descending-neuron site and a nerve-cord motor site; what survives calcium imaging blur and slow frame rates; how fast decoding degrades when a probe misses its target by tens of microns.

      What we predict

      From the preregistration draft, which is not yet tagged; the pilot rides above started before the tag, so they are reported as pilots and the confirmatory runs use readouts and rewiring draws the pilots never saw: R1 and R2 ride within one seed-SD of the real wiring. R3 loses under 30%, and the transition lies between R3 and the strength-preserving R3.5. R4, recalibrated to its own synaptic strength, rides no better than the sensory bypass. If R4 rides as well as R0, a trained decoder can extract the behaviour from scrambled wiring, closed-loop behaviour is not a valid test of an emulation, and we release the decoder-free battery as the test the field should use instead. Negative results get published as prominently as positive ones.
