Scrambled Brains

How accurate must a wiring map be before a brain built from it keeps its behaviour? We run a whole fruit-fly nervous system from its connectome, teach it to ride a bicycle, then corrupt the wiring step by step, from realistic mapping errors to random wiring, and measure where the riding breaks.

  • 166,700 neurons, brain and nerve cord
  • 25.6 M connections, 124 M synapses
  • wiring ladder R0 to R5
  • one task the bike cannot do by itself

The question

Whole-brain emulation assumes that a scanned wiring map plus a neuron model reproduces behaviour. Proofreading the map is projected to dominate the cost of future connectomes, yet nobody has measured how much wiring accuracy a closed-loop behaviour actually needs.

The sharpest objection is the "digital sphinx": Brunton et al. (eLife 2026) trained a decoder that let a 302-neuron worm connectome drive a walking fly body, and argued that a random network would have served as well. If a trained decoder can extract behaviour from scrambled wiring, closed-loop behaviour is not a valid test of an emulation at all.

So we grade the fly's wiring from real to random and score every rung on identical tasks: riding a bicycle below the speed at which it balances itself, the model's published decoder-free predictions, and how many neurons a decoder must read. The result is a fidelity curve: an acceptance test any connectome or emulator can be run against.

The wiring ladder

Every rung keeps the neurons and each connection's sender, so out-degrees stay where they are. Transmitter signs stay too on every rung but R1, which flips the sign of 5% of the neurons. What else survives shrinks rung by rung. Generators and tests: fly-brain, brain/variants.py.

Results so far

Three things so far. The real wiring can balance the bike below its self-stable speed, so the task is not solved by the bicycle. The pre-test shows which rungs still carry a left-right signal, and it puts the break between two rungs that differ only in who a neuron's partners are. And the first pilot rides on rewired brains are in, each of R1, R2 and R3 on two rewiring draws: R3, which keeps only which cell types connect to which, learns to ride; its median over four searches is 7% below the real wiring's. R1, which keeps the wiring and turns 5% of the neurons from excitatory to inhibitory or the reverse, ends 45% below the real wiring in one search and 4% below in a second search on the same rewiring draw, and 8% below on a second draw. The first search goes against a prediction in our preregistration draft, the other two are inside it; since the second search used the same brain and the same lane filter, the first was a search that stalled, not a property of the brain. Replayed, that stalled decoder still balances; what it loses is the road. R2, which keeps the wiring and jitters every synapse count by a factor of about 0.8 to 1.25, ends its three searches on two draws at 13.4, 13.5 and 12.0 s. A third search on the real wiring itself, run on 7 November, came out below its first two, so the real wiring's own spread is wider than we had. Over all their searches, against the mean of the real wiring's three (12.2 s), R2 is 6% above, R3 14% below and R1 15% below; the last two each include one stalled search. Every pilot but those two stalled searches (3.6 and 2.6 SD below) is within one seed-SD. R4 at 1.2 did not learn. Four more pilots on the real wiring are in as well, one seed each. They change only which neurons the decoder reads. Reading the 8 or the 32 descending neurons that follow the bike's roll rate most closely (top-8 and top-32) keeps the bike upright but not on the road. Reading 8 descending neurons drawn at random does not even balance: that search ended at 4.1 s, below the bike with no steering, and when its final decoder is replayed without the road-edge rule all 48 riders fall. A decoder that skips the brain and reads the senses rides 13.9 s, about what the better of the balance gate's two searches at the same speed ended on (13.7 s).

    2. The transmission pre-test: do scrambled brains still carry a left-right signal?

    Before any rung gets search compute, we hold the bike upright, drive every sense at cruise rate, and add 30 Hz to the left or the right vertical-system (VS) or haltere inputs, in 30 brains per cell. A cell passes when, for both probes, more descending and motor neurons tell left from right (|z| > 3) than in any of 1,000 shuffled groupings, at a network rate within 2× of the real brain's (10,750 to 43,000 spikes/s). The synapse scale multiplies Shiu's weight; 0.47 is the male CNS calibration.

    The real wiring and the three mildest rungs pass at the calibrated scale: transmitter errors (R1), count jitter (R2) and type-preserving rewiring (R3) give 31 to 50 lateralised outputs where shuffled groupings give at most 13. R3 does it at 73% of the real network rate, so wiring specified only down to cell types still carries the bike's left-right signal. One step down, R3.5 gives every neuron the right number of inputs, the right input strength and the right excitatory/inhibitory mix, from the wrong partners, and fails at every scale we ran. Degree-preserving wiring (R4) and random wiring (R5) are silent at the calibrated scale. R4 carries a signal at 1.5 times Shiu's weight but stays just under the rate window; R5 passes at twice Shiu's weight with a weak signal (7 and 8 outputs; the real wiring has 38 and 39). That pass contradicts our draft prediction that random wiring fails everywhere. One rewiring draw per rung. Cells marked 8 are the earlier 8-brain pilot.

    Between the ladder steps: is a failing rung only miscalibrated?

    Not on this evidence, for R3.5 (nine scales, one rewiring draw). At 0.50 its network rate is inside the window, about three quarters of the real brain's, and it still carries no left-right signal: one lateralised output per probe, while single brains burst so much that shuffled groupings reach 160. Both probes beat the shuffles only from 0.6, at more than five times the real rate. R4 has the opposite problem: a clear signal from 1.2 to 1.5 at a fraction of the real rate, then runaway activity at 1.6.

    The pre-test holds the bike upright, and riding drives the senses harder. Over the first 0.6 s after setting off, with nearly every rider still up, the real wiring's activity roughly doubles. R4's rises 10 to 22 times at scales 1.0 to 1.3, and above that it runs away: at 1.5 every R4 brain reached 4 million spikes/s within a second, each before its own bike fell. So a rung's synaptic scale has to be checked while riding, not only upright. R4's pilot ride runs at 1.2, the highest scale in this scan within 2× of the real rate. It is not matched throughout: averaged over the first generations of the search, where most bikes have already fallen, R4 at 1.2 fires about three times as much as the real wiring.

    3. Riding on rewired brains: first pilot runs

    The pilot is the balance gate unchanged, same hand-picked readout, search and seed, on rewiring draw 1, so its curves compare directly with the real wiring's. The GPU node has had no working CUDA since its operating-system upgrade, so these runs are on CPU. R3 took 23 to 83 minutes per generation at first and 7 to 19 minutes since the evening of 5 November, when the simulator's step got a C version that gives the same spikes bit for bit (two generations right after the switch took 25 and 47 minutes); on the GPU a generation of the real wiring took about 5 minutes. A search continues from one 24-hour job to the next. The figure grows as generations finish.

Whether R1's brain carried too little of the lane to those neurons, or the lane filter built for R1, or this one search was at fault, the two later searches below answered: it was the search. The real wiring's two decoders keep 46 and 44 of 48 on the road for the full 15 s (37 and 25 in the last generation), R3's two keep 22 and 28 (20 and 25), and R1's keeps 10 (4).       Riding on a screened readout: pilot on the real wiring

      Every ride above reads a hand-picked list of 42 cell types, 35 descending and 7 motor, plus one lane signal from a matched filter over the same neurons. On a scrambled brain a cell type's name no longer says what its neurons do, so the confirmatory runs give every brain its own passenger screen: a simple controller steers the bike while the brain watches through its senses, and the decoder then reads the 8 or 32 descending neurons whose firing follows the bike's roll rate most closely. The neurons are picked for roll rate only and the decoder has no lane filter, so a rider could balance and still leave the road. Two controls bracket it: the same number of neurons drawn at random from the same pool, and a decoder that skips the brain and reads the ten sensory populations directly. Whether the screened readout can ride at all is piloted on the real wiring first: the top-8 readout, the bypass, the top-32 readout and 8 neurons drawn at random, 30 generations each, one seed.

        Two thirds of the top-8 decoder's steering weight is on one pair of neurons, DNp20 left against right. So eight neurons picked for roll rate are enough to balance, and what that readout lacks is the lane. In the top-32 search, more neurons of the same kind did not supply it. The bypass shows that the same search holds the road when what the decoder reads carries the bike's position on the road. The senses do carry it: the bike's heading and its distance from the center line are fed directly to two of the ten sensory populations, the left and right horizontal-system (HS) visual cells. The bypass also shows that the senses alone are enough for this task, so it can serve as the ceiling it was planned to be: the line a readout from the brain's output neurons is measured against. We checked whether the bypass depends on the brain's wiring. The sensory neurons also receive synapses from the rest of the brain, so we repeated the same search with every synapse switched off. That search stays within 0.1 s of the bypass in every one of its 30 generations and ends at 13.9 s, with 81% riding the full 15 s against 77% for the bypass. So the bypass rides as well without the wiring. The hand-picked readout differs from the screened ones in more than one way: it reads 42 cell types, nine of them picked for the lane and not for roll rate, it has a lane signal from a matched filter, and its search is set up differently. Ranking the same screen by the bike's distance from the center line is not an obvious fix: no descending neuron in it follows that distance closely (the best correlation is 0.29, against 0.65 for roll rate).

        The control with 8 descending neurons drawn at random never reached the hands-off level (5.4 to 5.7 s). From generation 3 on its search stayed between 3.5 and 4.6 s and ended at 4.1 s. Of its 1,440 rides (30 generations of 48), one lasted the full 15 s. The decoder it ended on is close to blank. The draw is not a silent one: before the ride starts, five of its 8 neurons fire at 0.5 Hz or more, against six of the top 8. Replayed with 48 riders and without the search's random variation, the decoder rides about like a bike with no steering. None rides the full 15 s, 38 leave the road and 10 fall (5.5 s on average); with no steering, 41 leave the road and 7 fall (5.4 s). Without the rule that ends a ride at the road edge, all 48 fall (7.8 s on average); with no steering, 46 of 48 do (8.6 s).         Three ways to score a rung

        Track A · closed-loop riding

        A decoder learned from blank weights (cross-entropy method, identical seeds for every rung) reads the k descending neurons that the brain's own passenger screen ranks highest, k = 8 and 32, with a random-k control. Reported as the gap to two reference lines: a decoder that bypasses the brain and reads the senses directly (ceiling), and the hands-off bike (floor).

        Track B · decoder-free predictions

        Feeding predictions from Shiu et al., and a few looming-escape predictions from flybench, rerun on every rung with no trained readout. Adds transmitter errors at Eckstein et al.'s measured confusion rates as a control the field does not yet have. Pilot, 9 November: six scored predictions, each checked against the paper it cites, at scale 0.47 with both sides of the taste input driven. A row counts if its sign is right and its size is at least half the real wiring's. R1 to R3 draws score 1.0, except R1 draw 1 at 0.33, the draw with almost no sugar response, and R2 draw 2 at 0.83. R3.5, R4 and R5 score 0. The criterion, the list of rows and the two-sided drive were chosen after a first pilot on a draft list, and two rows that failed on the real wiring were dropped then, so this is not blind.

        Track C · decodability

        How many recorded neurons a decoder needs to reach 90% of the full roll-rate R² (N90), at a brain descending-neuron site and a nerve-cord motor site; what survives calcium imaging blur and slow frame rates; how fast decoding degrades when a probe misses its target by tens of microns. First pilot, 8 November, one replay per brain with the hand-picked readout: on R0, R1, R2 and R3 the three neurons the screen ranks highest reach 90% of what all descending neurons give (R² 0.93 to 0.96 for all of them), where neurons drawn at random from the site need about 250 to 450 (about 60 to 100 if only the neurons active in that replay are drawn). So N90 at this site does not separate these rungs. No rung past R3 has a decoder to replay yet: R3.5 and R5 have not been ridden, and the R4 pilot did not learn.

        What we predict

        From the preregistration draft, which is not yet tagged; the pilot rides above started before the tag, so they are reported as pilots. On rewired brains the confirmatory runs read each brain's own screened neurons, which no pilot has ridden on a rewired brain, and include rewiring draws the pilots never saw; the screened readout and the sensory bypass are piloted beforehand on the real wiring only: R1's and R2's median riding time over the five search seeds lies within one seed-SD of the real wiring's (restated on 8 November from "R1 and R2 ride within one seed-SD", so that one stalled search cannot decide it; with the hand-picked readout the pilots' medians are R1 11.8 s, inside, and R2 13.4 s, 1.1 SD above and so just outside, against the real wiring's 11.9 s with SD 1.4 s; two and three seeds, not the test). R3 loses under 30%, and the transition lies between R3 and the strength-preserving R3.5. R4, recalibrated to its own synaptic strength, rides no better than the random-k control does on the real wiring. If R4 rides as well as R0, a trained decoder can extract the behaviour from scrambled wiring, closed-loop behaviour is not a valid test of an emulation, and we release the decoder-free battery as the test the field should use instead. Negative results get published as prominently as positive ones.
